Walk through Pilot 1

Pilot 1 opens Entrepalooza, a small, human-guided cohort in Houston for adults figuring out what comes next. Here's how it feels, one step at a time.

Public preview: exploring this workbook is not enrollment in the cohort.

How this works

Notice → Try → Reflect → Decide. You notice what has your attention, try something small, look at what happened, and choose what makes sense next.

Conversation Journal: use it when you want to keep a thought or answer. It is optional, and nothing is saved automatically or sent to Entrepalooza.

You can skip a question, pass on an activity, plan a try for later, or stop and come back.

Before we start, are you here?

What the workbook keeps track of

Your answers build three running tools. You do not fill them out separately.

  • What I’m noticing: interests, tries, and things you learned.
  • What helps me work: conditions, supports, limits, and barriers you noticed.
  • My next choice: the decision you make after a try.
Your choices after a try

Keep · Change · Test Again · Pause/Park · Stop · Another Direction · Continue Looking. None is treated as the “right” finish.

Reading 1 — You Donʼt Have to Know Yet

There is a lot of pressure to figure out what you are passionate about.

Find your calling.

Choose your direction.

Know what you want.

Build the plan.

That sounds clean.

Real life often is not.

Sometimes you do know.

Sometimes you have a strong pull toward something and cannot stop thinking

about it.

Sometimes you have a few loose threads.

And sometimes the most accurate answer is:

I donʼt know yet.

That is not a failed answer.

Interest does not always arrive fully formed

Research on interest development gives us a useful correction to the idea that

everyone has one hidden passion waiting to be discovered.

Interest can begin as something small.

Something catches your attention.

You encounter it again.

You learn a little more.

You become more involved.

Sometimes that interest disappears.

Sometimes it sticks around.

Sometimes it deepens.

The important word is can.

Seeing something once does not mean it will become meaningful.

Being curious does not mean you have discovered your future.

But you also do not have to wait for a lightning-bolt moment before you explore.

Look for signals, not answers

Instead of asking:

“What is my passion?”

start smaller.

What keeps catching your attention?

What do you keep looking up?

What kinds of problems do you notice?

What do you complain about because you wish someone would fix it?

What work do you find yourself watching other people do?

What have you returned to more than once?

What did you care about before somebody told you whether it was useful?

What do people tend to ask you for help with?

What kind of environment makes you curious?

What question keeps coming back?

None of those things proves that you have found “the thing.”

They are signals.

Signals are worth investigating.

They are not instructions.

Interest is not destiny

Something can interest you and still be wrong for you.

You can enjoy learning about a field and hate doing the work.

You can admire a profession and discover that its daily reality does not fit your life.

You can be good at something and not want to build your future around it.

You can care deeply about something without turning it into a career or business.

And an interest can matter for a while and then change.

Exploration is not a contract.

It is how you get better information.

You are allowed to begin without certainty

If you already have something you want to explore, use it.

If you have three possibilities, good.

If all you have is:

“I know I donʼt want things to stay the same, but I donʼt know what comes next.”

you can start there too.

Your job in this module is not to identify your permanent purpose.

It is to find something with enough pull, curiosity, frustration, or possibility that it

deserves a closer look.

Carry this question forward

What has enough of my attention that I would be willing to learn a little more about

it?

Attention, curiosity, friction, and repeated questions can provide starting information without becoming commitments.

Fictional example: A person keeps noticing community gardens but does not know whether they want to garden, volunteer, learn about food systems, meet people, or simply spend time outside.

Choose one equal entry route

“I don’t know yet” is a valid completion state. “Nothing clear yet” is sufficient. No writing or disclosure is required for any route.

Every response is optional. You may pass.

Lesson 2 · 3 of 7

Obsessive and harmonious passion (patterns, not labels)

Your work stays in this tab unless you choose to save or download it. Nothing you write is sent to Entrepalooza. Keep it private, use a fictional example, observe, pass, or return later. No personal disclosure is required.

Reading 2 — When Something Matters, How It Holds You Matters

Caring intensely about something is not automatically a problem.

It is also not automatically healthy.

Research on passion makes a distinction that is useful here.

It is not simply:

passion or no passion.

It also matters how the passion is held.

Researchers studying what is called the Dualistic Model of Passion describe two

broad patterns: harmonious passion and obsessive passion.

These are research concepts.

They are not personality types.

This module is not going to label you as one or the other.

Harmonious passion

Harmonious passion describes a pattern where an activity matters deeply but still

feels more freely chosen.

The activity can become an important part of your life without taking over all of it.

You may work hard.

You may care a lot.

You may spend serious time on it.

But there is usually more room for flexibility.

You can adjust.

You can make space for other parts of life.

You can sometimes stop without feeling that stopping destroys who you are.

Obsessive passion

Obsessive passion describes a more pressured relationship with an activity.

The activity may still be something you genuinely love.

But continuing can feel less optional.

Pressure may come from inside:

“I have to prove myself.”

“If I stop, Iʼm failing.”

“This is what makes me valuable.”

Or it may be tied to approval, status, expectations, or other demands.

The research describes this pattern as involving more internal pressure and rigid

persistence.

That does not mean every intense effort is obsessive.

It does not mean working long hours automatically tells us what kind of passion is

present.

And it does not mean a person belongs permanently in one category.

The difference is not how much you care

Two people can care about the same activity just as intensely and have very

different relationships with it.

One person may feel:

“I choose this. It matters to me. I can work hard and still change course when I

need to.”

Another may feel:

“I have to do this. If I slow down, Iʼm failing. If this does not work, I donʼt know

what that says about me.”

Real life can be mixed.

You may feel free and excited one day and pressured the next.

The activity itself matters.

So can the reasons you are doing it, the conditions around it, and what else is

happening in your life.

This module is not asking you to diagnose the pattern.

It is asking you to notice it.

The goal is not to kill the fire

Intensity can build things.

It can also burn through everything around it.

The point is not to become less driven.

It is to learn enough about your relationship with that drive that you can make

better decisions about where to put it.

So instead of asking only:

“How passionate am I?”

also ask:

“Do I still feel able to choose?”

“Can I adjust?”

“Can I stop when I need to?”

“What happens to the rest of my life when I pursue this?”

Those questions do not tell you whether an activity is good or bad.

They give you more information about how you are engaging with it.

Carry this question forward

When I care intensely about something, what helps me stay able to choose

instead of feeling controlled by it?

Obsessive passion is a pattern in which an activity may feel controlling, rigid, pressured, or tied to self-worth. Harmonious passion is a pattern in which an activity matters deeply while remaining chosen, flexible, and able to fit alongside other parts of life. Harmonious does not mean weak, effortless, or free of difficulty.

Fictional pattern A: A person enjoys making music, can pause when needed, adjusts expectations, and remains connected to sleep, relationships, and responsibilities.

Fictional pattern B: A person feels unable to stop making music, treats every interruption as a threat, neglects essential needs, and believes poor performance means personal failure.

Neither person receives a diagnosis or identity label. These are patterns that may change with conditions over time. Never call a person “obsessive.”

What to notice — no score or cutoff
Try—or pass
What pattern do you notice?

Mixed and Unclear are complete answers. One module does not diagnose or convert a passion type.

Reading 3 — A Try Is Not a Commitment

Sometimes exploration gets crushed under the weight of imaginary permanence.

You look into a career and suddenly it feels like you are choosing what you will do

for the next thirty years.

You consider a class and your brain jumps to a degree.

You think about selling something and suddenly you are imagining a company,

taxes, investors, failure, and a logo you do not even have.

The possibility becomes enormous before you have learned much about it.

So we are going to separate two things:

exploring

and

committing.

They are not the same decision.

Different phases ask different questions

Research on goal pursuit distinguishes between deciding what to pursue and

carrying out a decision once it has been made.

Those phases can involve different kinds of thinking.

When you are still exploring, your job is not to defend a decision you have not

made.

Your job is to gather information.

What is this actually like?

What pulls me in?

What pushes me away?

What surprised me?

What would I need to learn?

What barriers are real?

What assumptions was I making?

Only later might the question become:

“Do I want to commit more seriously to this?”

Make the next move smaller than the future

In Entrepalooza, we use bounded tries.

That is a program design choice.

It means you decide in advance how large the exploration is allowed to become.

You might set:

a time limit

a money limit

an energy limit

a privacy limit

a support you want available

something you are not willing to do

a reason you would pause

a reason you would stop

a point when you will review what happened

The purpose is not to make the try scientifically perfect.

It is to keep exploration from quietly turning into a commitment you never chose.

Build the exit before you enter

Suppose you want to know whether photography interests you enough to explore

further.

You do not need to buy professional equipment.

Your try might be:

“For one week, Iʼll use the camera I already have and spend thirty minutes on

three different days learning and practicing.”

Or maybe you are curious about a trade.

The next move does not have to be:

“Enroll in school.”

It might be:

“Talk to one person who actually does the work and watch one credible

demonstration of what the job involves.”

Or maybe you are wondering whether people would pay for something you can

make.

You do not need to launch a business.

You might create one rough version and ask for feedback.

The try should match what you actually need to learn.

A useful try can disappoint you

A try is not successful only when it makes you want more.

You may discover:

“I liked the idea more than the actual work.”

“I enjoyed this, but not enough to build my life around it.”

“I want to keep exploring, but I need different conditions.”

“The work interests me, but the cost or schedule does not fit my reality.”

“I thought I would hate this and I didnʼt.”

“I still donʼt know.”

Those are all possible outcomes.

One try cannot tell you everything.

It can give you another piece of evidence.

That is enough for the next decision.

Do not turn five minutes of information into a five-year decision

Exploration works better when the question is the right size.

Not:

“Is this my future?”

Try:

“Is this worth another look?”

Not:

“Should I become this kind of person?”

Try:

“What would I learn from one manageable step?”

You are allowed to gather evidence before demanding certainty from yourself.

Carry this question forward

What could I try without needing the try to become my future?

Reading 1 — Maybe Itʼs Not Just You. Look at the Setup.

From Module 1 to here

In Module 1, you explored something that caught your attention and started

noticing how you related to it.

Maybe it felt chosen.

Maybe it felt pressured.

Maybe you could stop easily.

Maybe stopping was harder than you expected.

Maybe you liked the activity but not the way you were doing it.

Maybe the whole thing was mixed or unclear.

Module 1 did not ask you to decide:

“This is just who I am.”

Module 1.5 picks up there.

Before you explain the experience entirely through your personality, motivation,

ability, or the activity itself, there is another part of the picture worth examining:

the conditions around what you were doing.

Research on behavior and personality has repeatedly shown that people do not

behave exactly the same way in every situation. What someone does can vary

depending on the situation, even while people also show real patterns across time.

So the question here is not:

“Was it me or the environment?”

Usually, life is not that clean.

A better question is:

“What part of the person, task, and situation might be affecting what happened?”

That is the job of this module.

You will look at the setup around an activity, change one manageable part when

possible, and see whether anything worth noticing changes.

People turn experiences into descriptions of

themselves quickly

Iʼm lazy.

Iʼm bad at this.

I canʼt focus.

I work better alone.

I need pressure.

Iʼm terrible with structure.

I never finish anything.

Sometimes those conclusions may describe a real pattern.

But one experience rarely tells you the whole story.

Imagine trying to read while three people are talking, your phone keeps buzzing,

you are hungry, and you have twenty minutes before you need to leave.

Now imagine reading similar material somewhere quiet, after eating, with more

time available and your phone out of reach.

Same person.

Similar task.

Different setup.

Your experience might change.

Or it might not.

Either result is useful because it reminds you that the person may not be the only

relevant factor.

Every activity happens under conditions

Nobody acts in a vacuum.

There is always a situation around what you are doing.

There may be:

a deadline

no deadline

clear instructions

confusing instructions

more or less choice

noise

silence

another person nearby

someone evaluating the work

nobody available to help

interruptions

time pressure

unfamiliar technology

transportation problems

childcare demands

predictable routines

uncertainty

encouragement

criticism

money pressure

physical discomfort

exhaustion before you begin

In Entrepalooza, we call parts of this setup operating conditions.

That is a program term.

An operating condition is simply something about the situation surrounding an

activity that may be relevant to how the activity goes.

It is not a diagnosis.

It is not a personality type.

And noticing a condition does not prove that the condition caused what happened.

It gives you something worth examining.

Choice and structure can both matter

One reason we look at conditions is that motivation research shows that social

environments can matter.

For example, research on autonomy support has found that people tend to show

more autonomous motivation and engagement when they experience meaningful

choice, perspective-taking, and less controlling pressure.

Research also suggests that structure and autonomy do not have to be opposites.

Clear expectations, useful information, and boundaries can support people

without removing choice.

That matters here because:

“I need structure”

and

“I need freedom”

may be too crude.

A person might need:

enough structure to understand what they are doing and enough choice to still

feel ownership of it.

That is something you can investigate.

Different does not mean defective

Suppose you begin more easily when another person is nearby.

That does not automatically mean you are dependent.

Suppose written instructions are easier to use than six spoken directions.

That does not prove anything is wrong with you.

Suppose you work differently with music, silence, a timer, a deadline, a break,

more choice, or clearer structure.

That is information about what happened under those conditions.

The trap is turning the result into another permanent rule:

“This is the only way I can function.”

Maybe that pattern will show up repeatedly.

Maybe it will not.

Different tasks and situations can produce different results.

This module is not trying to uncover your permanent work style.

It is helping you examine patterns without declaring them permanent too early.

Observation first

Consider:

Observation:

“I worked on it for three hours and skipped lunch.”

Now consider:

Interpretation:

“I have no self-control.”

The first describes something that happened.

The second explains it.

The explanation could eventually contain something useful.

But the observation alone does not establish why it happened.

Other possibilities could include:

you lost track of time

the activity was unusually engaging

there was no stopping point

you were under a deadline

you worried you would not restart if you stopped

food was not easily available

several factors were operating at once

You do not have to choose the “nice” explanation.

You just do not need to pretend you know more than you know.

First:

What happened?

Then:

What might explain it?

Context is not an excuse

Looking at conditions does not erase responsibility.

If you made a choice that caused a problem, that still matters.

But accurate learning usually requires more than asking:

“What is wrong with me?”

Look at:

person + task + situation.

That gives you more to work with.

Carry this question forward

What part of the setup around this activity might be worth examining before I

decide what the experience says about me?

An operating condition is one part of the setup around an activity. An observation describes what happened. An interpretation is an explanation of why it happened. One try is a data point, not a profile.

Bring one observation or question you choose from Module 1. You do not have to repeat personal information or use an emotionally meaningful activity. “Nothing is clear yet; I want to use a neutral task” is valid.

Try—or pass

Observation: “I wrote for three hours and skipped lunch.”

Interpretation: “I have no self-control.”

The observation is evidence. The interpretation may need more information. Losing track of time, an engaging activity, no stopping point, a deadline, worry about restarting, or food not being available could also matter. You do not have to choose the “nice” explanation. First: what happened? Then: what might explain it?

Notice whether either makes the next step clearer, feels controlling, or makes no difference. Neither format is universally better. You may observe the example without doing the task.

Lesson 2 of 3

Your work stays in this tab unless you choose to save or download it. Nothing you write is sent to Entrepalooza. Use a neutral example, keep it private, observe, pass, or return later. No disclosure is required.

Reading 2 — Change One Thing and See What You Learn

You have looked at the setup.

Now you are going to make the question smaller.

Not:

“What environment is perfect for me?”

Not:

“How do I finally fix the way I work?”

Instead:

“What happens if I change one part of the setup?”

Entrepalooza calls this a changed-condition trial.

It is not a scientific experiment.

Real life has too many moving pieces for that.

It is a structured way to gather better information.

Start with something you noticed

Maybe you noticed:

I have trouble stopping once I start.

I keep getting pulled away by my phone.

I freeze when the instructions are vague.

I start more easily when someone else is working nearby.

I rush when I can see the clock.

I lose interest when the task feels heavily controlled.

I do better when I know what “done” means.

Instead of turning that observation into a rule, turn it into a question.

Instead of:

“I need pressure to work.”

Try:

“What happens if I give myself a clear stopping time?”

Instead of:

“I canʼt focus around people.”

Try:

“What do I notice if I do a similar task somewhere quieter?”

Instead of:

“I always lose track of time.”

Try:

“What happens if I decide when I will stop before I begin?”

The question creates room for evidence.

Change as little as practical

Suppose you want to know whether a visible stopping point affects the way you

work.

Try the activity with one.

If possible, avoid completely redesigning everything else at the same time.

Why?

Because if you change the room, the task, the schedule, the person helping you,

the music, the instructions, and the time limit all at once, you may learn:

“This version felt different.”

But you will have less information about what contributed to the difference.

You do not need laboratory control.

You are simply making the comparison easier to understand.

Decide what you want to notice

A changed condition can affect different things.

So choose what matters for your question.

You might watch for:

how easy it was to begin

whether you stayed with the task

whether you understood what to do

how much pressure you felt

whether you could stop when you planned

how much effort it took

whether you wanted to return

how you felt afterward

Do not try to measure everything.

A useful question is:

“If this condition mattered, what difference might I actually notice?”

Decide the boundaries before you begin

Entrepalooza uses boundaries for these trials.

That is a program design choice.

You might decide:

how long you are willing to try

how much money, if any, you are willing to spend

what support you want available

what would make you pause

what would make you stop

For example:

“Iʼll try this for twenty minutes.”

“Iʼm not spending money on this trial.”

“Iʼll ask for clarification if I stay stuck for ten minutes.”

“Iʼll stop if I am no longer learning anything useful.”

Planning a response in advance can sometimes make it easier to act when the

situation actually arrives. Research on implementation intentions supports the

usefulness of linking a recognizable situation with a planned response.

But the specific boundaries used here belong to our trial design.

Their purpose is simple:

keep the try small enough that you remain able to choose.

Then see what happens

Change the condition.

Reading 1: What Happened Is Not the Same as What It Means

From Module 1.5 to here

In Module 1.5, you looked at what happened when you changed part of the setup

around an activity.

Maybe the change helped. Maybe it did not. Maybe the result was mixed. Maybe

you never had the chance to try.

Now another process begins: making sense of what happened.

That is normal. People interpret events. We explain them. We connect them to

things we already believe. Sometimes that helps us learn. Sometimes the

explanation becomes bigger than the evidence.

Module 2 is about slowing that down.

Event, story, verdict

Entrepalooza uses three words as a teaching tool:

Event: What happened that can be observed or checked.

Story: The explanation or meaning attached to what happened.

Verdict: A broader judgment about what the event says about you.

This is not a claim that every thought happens in three neat stages. Real thinking

is messier than that. The point of the tool is to create enough space to ask: What

do I actually know?

Consider:

Event: “I stopped after twenty minutes.”

Story: “I stopped because I cannot handle difficult things.”

Verdict: “I never follow through.”

The event happened. The story might be right, partly right, or wrong. The verdict

makes an even larger claim. The larger the claim becomes, the more evidence it

needs.

Explanations matter

Psychological research has long examined the way people interpret events. One

useful finding is that explanations for negative events can differ in how broad or

permanent they become.

For example, there is a difference between “I handled this situation badly” and “I

am bad at everything.”

Research on attributional patterns, especially in depression, has found

associations between negative events being explained in broad, stable, self-

focused ways and depressive symptoms.

That does not mean having one thought like this means you are depressed. And

this module is not diagnosing anyone. It gives us a reason to be careful about

turning one event into a permanent conclusion about the entire person.

Accuracy is not positive thinking

Suppose you missed an important commitment. The goal is not to transform “I

missed it” into “Actually, Iʼm amazing.” That would not be more accurate.

A more useful statement might be:

“I missed the commitment. That affected someone else. I need to address it,

and I want to understand what contributed to it so I can respond differently next

time.”

That statement keeps responsibility. It also stays close to the evidence.

Context and responsibility can exist together

Looking at context does not erase what you did. Suppose you missed a deadline.

Relevant information might include:

You misunderstood the instructions.

You waited too long to begin.

You avoided asking for clarification.

Your childcare fell through.

Technology failed.

You underestimated the amount of work.

You were exhausted.

Several things happened at once.

You do not have to choose between “It was entirely my fault” and “Nothing was

my responsibility.” A more accurate picture can contain both what I was

responsible for and what else affected the situation. That gives you more options

for what to change.

Contrary evidence matters too

Suppose your verdict is “I never finish anything.” Before treating that as

established fact, ask:

Have I finished anything similar before?

Does this happen in every setting?

Were there times when support changed the outcome?

Are there examples that do not fit the word never?

Contrary evidence does not automatically erase the problem. It helps determine

how large the claim deserves to be.

Maybe the more accurate version is “I have a pattern of not finishing some

projects once they become difficult.” That may still be uncomfortable. But now it is

specific enough to investigate.

“I donʼt know” can be accurate

Sometimes you cannot explain the event yet. You can say:

“I stopped after twenty minutes. I was frustrated and tired. I do not know yet

whether the main issue was the activity, the conditions, my preparation, or

something else.”

Uncertainty can be frustrating. But pretending certainty does not create better

evidence.

Carry this question forward

What happened, what meaning did I attach to it, and how much of that meaning

does the evidence actually support?

Event: what happened that can be observed or checked. Story: the meaning or explanation attached to the event. Verdict: a broad judgment about who the person is. Uncertainty: what remains unknown. The goal is accuracy, not forced positivity. Responsibility and context can both remain in the picture.

Neutral example: Event — “The person stopped after 20 minutes.” Story — “They stopped because they cannot handle difficult things.” Verdict — “They never follow through.” A more accurate revision could acknowledge a boundary, barrier, or setup without making a permanent identity claim.

There is research behind the idea of testing beliefs through action instead of only

look for a small, safe way to gather new information.

This is an educational adaptation. It is not CBT treatment.

One observable action is enough. You are not trying to prove “I have completely

information about what you did in a particular situation.

There is not one strategy that is universally best in every situation.

partly on the person, the situation, the options available, and what happens after a

strategy is used.

So this module does not teach:

“Whenever intensity rises, always do X.”

It teaches:

notice → choose a reasonable response → watch what happens.

Choose what you are protecting

What must not get bulldozed while I pursue this?

Maybe:

“My sleep.”

“Picking up my kid.”

“My rent money.”

“A relationship.”

“My recovery time.”

“Another commitment.”

“My ability to stop.”

“My physical safety.”

“My privacy.”

The protected boundary is an Entrepalooza curriculum tool.

Research did not hand us a universal list of boundaries.

The point of the tool is to make an important limit visible before intensity makes

the decision for you.

Then look at the result

If you try a response, notice:

Did the next choice become easier?

Harder?

Unchanged?

Unclear?

What did the response require?

What happened afterward?

What else may have influenced the result?

One attempt does not prove the response works universally.

It gives you another piece of evidence.

You can also choose not to try

Maybe the response is not appropriate.

Maybe the barrier cannot be changed right now.

Maybe you need support first.

Maybe you want to stop instead.

Maybe continuing no longer makes sense.

Reading 3 — A Hunch Can Start a Question. It Cannot Finish One.

By now, you have collected several kinds of information.

You have explored what catches your attention.

You have tested conditions.

You have examined stories.

You have watched what happens when intensity changes.

Now you may have an impression.

“I think this could really fit me.”

“Something about this feels wrong.”

“I think I should keep going.”

“I have a feeling I could make this work.”

“I donʼt think this is for me.”

Those impressions matter.

But they are not the same thing as evidence.

Intuition can be useful

Research on judgment makes this more complicated than:

“Trust your gut”

or

“Never trust your gut.”

Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein, researchers who came from very different

traditions, found substantial agreement on this point:

Intuition can become highly skilled when someone operates in an environment

with learnable patterns and has enough opportunity for meaningful feedback and

practice.

In other situations, intuitive judgments can be confidently wrong.

Most importantly:

feeling certain is not proof that the judgment is accurate.

So Entrepalooza treats intuition as a question generator.

If your gut says:

“This fits me.”

ask:

“What evidence is making it feel that way?”

If it says:

“This will never work.”

ask:

“What evidence supports that, what challenges it, and what do I still not know?”

You do not have to ignore the hunch.

Make it earn its place beside the rest of the evidence.

Look at what the activity actually demands

Something can interest you and still have demands that do not fit your current

situation.

In this module, operating demands means what the activity actually asks from

you.

That might include:

time

attention

repeated decisions

uncertainty

interaction with other people

physical effort

money

transportation

technology

learning

schedule flexibility

recovery

administrative work

Do not evaluate only the exciting part.

And do not evaluate only the hardest part.

Look at the actual package.

Then create a decision rule

A decision rule is simply information you decide will matter when you make the

next choice.

For example:

“I will continue exploring if I can do this without spending more than $50 this

month.”

“I will test this again if I can arrange childcare first.”

“If I still cannot stop at the boundary I set after two more tries, I will pause and

reconsider the setup.”

“I will continue if the work itself still interests me after I talk to somebody who

actually does it.”

“If this repeatedly interferes with a responsibility I decided to protect, I will change

the plan rather than automatically pushing harder.”

The decision rule is a curriculum tool.

It helps move the next decision out of the fog of the moment.

Keep a protected boundary

Look back at what you said you wanted to protect.

You can keep it.

Change it.

Or decide that it is no longer relevant.

A protected boundary is not a promise that nothing difficult will happen.

It is a reminder that pursuing something meaningful does not require every other

part of life to become collateral damage.

Then make the next decision

Your options are:

Keep

Change

Test Again

Pause/Park

Stop

Another Direction

Continue Looking

There is no hidden correct answer.

Keep is not more successful than Stop.

Test Again is not more courageous than Another Direction.

Continue Looking is not a failure to decide.

The point is to make a choice that matches the evidence you have now.
